Storm warning for dogs who hate storms

Your dog already knows. Usually by shaking.

Thunder Dawg tells you about 36 minutes before that — while there is still time to do something about it.

The ladder

Four rungs, named for what TJ is doing about it.

No severity words. No “conditions favourable for convective initiation.” TJ is chillin, then he is on alert, then watching, then he wants in. Every rung carries his pose and a sentence, so none of it depends on you seeing the colour.

TJ is chillin Nothing out there. Nap weather. No strikes within 60 miles. Last one in the region: yesterday, 7:41 PM.
On alert Storms are possible later today. Storms are building southwest. No strikes anywhere yet — this is a forecast, not evidence.
Watching Thunder 38 miles southwest, closing. Moving your way at about 22 mph. Something to plan around.
Get TJ in Strikes 8 miles out. Bring the dogs in. Close enough to hear. Latest strike 6 minutes ago near Bainbridge.
Not watching Not watching right now. Last looked at the sky 47 minutes ago. This page is history until TJ wakes up. Silence is not a forecast.

That last one is not an error screen, it is a promise: a dead pipeline must never be allowed to look like a clear sky.

The odds

One in five. Not “18%.”

When TJ growls, the useful question is how often a storm like this one actually arrives. Four years of archive answers it — drawn as paws, because an icon array is the one risk format people reliably read correctly.

1 in 5 storms like this one reach us within two hours.

From 431 times a storm sat 30–50 miles out and closing, since 2022.

Never rounded to flatter. If the archive says one in five, one paw is filled. Where a cell is too thin to draw honestly the paws come off entirely, and the block says how many storms it actually saw instead.

Why nothing else does this

Every other app tells you it is already raining.

The National Weather Service only issues an alert when a storm turns severe. Ordinary thunderstorms — the exact ones dogs hate — get no official warning, ever. Everything downstream inherits that silence.

Apple Weather
Carries no lightning data at all. Not in conditions, not hourly, not in the precipitation list. There is nothing for it to tell you.
MyRadar
Says “lightning detected” — after. On 12 August it spoke at 4:44pm, once strikes were already within 20 km. The free NOAA grid had read 90% at 4:24pm.
Your dog
Extremely accurate. Terrible interface. By the time the alert arrives he is behind the toilet and it is too late for the chew.
Thunder Dawg
Watches the storm while it is still 25 miles out, along the line it is actually travelling, and says roughly when you will hear it.

And when he is chillin He means it. Across 43,473 samples where the lightning odds nearby sat under 5%, the chance of hearing thunder in the next 90 minutes was under one in a hundred. Silence that means something is the part no other app offers.

Things he is not good at

Better said now than discovered later.

A weather app that only advertises its wins is exactly the app that already lost your trust. So, plainly:

Can’t do it How hard it is going to rain

Testing was off by roughly double — sometimes too high, sometimes too low, on the same setup. Until that is rebuilt as odds rather than a number, no number gets quoted.

Still learning Storms that build directly overhead

The signal is there — 28 of 30 caught, about 38 minutes out — but not yet enough of it to know how often it would cry wolf. It runs quietly and says nothing until it has earned a voice.

Worth saying TJ reacts. He does not predict.

He growls because strikes already exist 25 miles away, not because a model believes something. That is far past hearing — thunder only carries 10 to 15 miles — so the warning is real. But it is “something is coming and here is when,” not a crystal ball.

On the record The storms he misses

Every one gets published, in the same size type as the ones he got right. The misses are the only reason to believe the hits.

Where this comes from

Built on NOAA’s public MRMS radar grid — MergedReflectivityQCComposite, PrecipRate and NLDN_CG — free, public domain, refreshed every two minutes. Anyone can download the same files. What takes four years is learning what they mean over one particular back yard, and keeping a public log of every call made in advance.
